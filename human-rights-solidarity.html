Human Rights Cannot Depend on Who Is Suffering

Why concern for minorities must cross borders and political loyalties

SS
Soumya Sharma25 October 2026 · 3 min read
THE GULABI PERSPECTIVE

Politics, through the everyday.

Explore more writing ↗

Public outrage can travel astonishing distances. A video from another country may provoke marches, statements and urgent calls for justice. Yet violence or intimidation closer to home, or across a neighbouring border, can receive far less attention. The unevenness raises an uncomfortable question: do we defend human rights as a principle, or do we defend them most loudly when the victims fit a cause we already support?

Concerns about the security of religious minorities in Bangladesh bring that question into sharp focus. Reports and allegations of attacks, threats or discrimination warrant careful investigation, protection for those at risk and an honest response from the authorities. They should not be reduced to material for rivalry between political camps in India. A person’s right to safety does not depend on whether the victim shares our religion, nationality or political outlook.

It is also important to resist a tempting shortcut: turning crimes committed by individuals or organised groups into a judgment on an entire faith. Religious identity does not establish personal guilt. Sweeping claims about Muslims, Hindus or any other community replace accountability with collective suspicion. That makes it harder to identify perpetrators, distinguish verified incidents from rumours and build solidarity with people who may themselves oppose violence.

The same principle applies to comparisons between Gaza, Bangladesh and other places where civilians face grave harm. These situations are not identical in history, scale, law or political context, and they should not be treated as interchangeable. But the fact that conflicts differ does not mean empathy must be exclusive. One can demand protection for Palestinian civilians while also insisting that Hindu, Buddhist, Christian and other minorities in Bangladesh enjoy equal rights and security. There is no contradiction in doing both.

Selective attention is not always proof of hypocrisy. People may know more about one crisis, belong to a movement connected to it or have limited time and reach. No individual can campaign on every injustice. Still, public figures, organisations and commentators who speak in universal terms should expect their principles to be tested across difficult cases. When allegations are inconvenient to one's preferred political narrative, that is precisely when a commitment to human rights matters most.

Responsible concern begins with accuracy. Images circulated during moments of unrest can be old, miscaptioned or stripped of context. Claims of persecution should be assessed through reliable reporting, independent documentation and, where possible, official investigations. Accuracy is not a reason to minimise suffering. It prevents genuine victims from becoming props in a cycle of misinformation and retaliation.

Governments, too, must be judged by consistent standards. Protecting minority citizens is a basic obligation, not a favour granted when international attention becomes intense. That requires effective policing, accountability for attacks, access to justice and political leadership that refuses to excuse intimidation. Neighbouring countries can raise concerns through diplomacy and humanitarian engagement without using vulnerable communities merely as symbols in domestic political arguments.

There is a difference between solidarity and spectacle. Solidarity listens to the people affected, asks what protection they need and supports institutions capable of providing it. Spectacle uses suffering to prove that another group is cruel or another ideology is dishonest. One may generate an immediate reaction online; the other has a better chance of helping people in real life.

Human rights will always be discussed in a world shaped by interests, loyalties and imperfect information. Consistency does not require identical reactions to every event. It requires the same moral starting point: violence against civilians is wrong, discrimination should be challenged and allegations must be examined fairly. A principle has little value if it disappears when the victim becomes politically inconvenient.
