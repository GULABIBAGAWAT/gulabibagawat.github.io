A Woman’s Right to the City Should Not End at Sunset

Gender, safety and the restrictions we have learned to call protection

SS
Soumya Sharma25 October 2026 · 3 min read
THE GULABI PERSPECTIVE

Politics, through the everyday.

Explore more writing ↗

A woman deciding whether to take an evening walk should not have to calculate which roads are safe, what time she must return or whether her clothes will invite comment. Yet for many women, ordinary choices are still treated as risks to be managed. A visit to a neighbourhood park, a walk home from work or a few minutes on the terrace can become the subject of family instructions and public scrutiny. The restrictions are often presented as concern for her safety. The effect, however, is to reduce her freedom.

There is a serious problem with a society that responds to the threat of harassment by regulating the movements of those who might face it. Women are asked to avoid certain places, dress differently, travel with someone or stay inside after dark. Meanwhile, the behaviour that makes those precautions necessary is too often accepted as inevitable. Safety is important, but safety without freedom cannot be the final goal. If the burden of preventing violence falls mainly on women, the social conditions that enable it remain largely untouched.

This contradiction extends into the workplace. Women who manage to move freely through education and employment may still confront assumptions about leadership, family responsibility and ambition. The term ‘glass ceiling’ describes the invisible barriers that slow advancement, while the ‘glass cliff’ points to the tendency in some settings to place women in precarious leadership roles when the likelihood of failure is high. These are not universal experiences, but they are useful ways of asking why access to an institution does not always mean equal power within it.

Domestic work reveals another imbalance. Paid employment has not automatically brought an equal sharing of household duties. Many women are expected to perform well at work while remaining the default caregivers at home. Motherhood may interrupt careers or affect how employers judge commitment. A promotion, a late meeting or travel for work can involve negotiations that many male colleagues are less often required to make. Equality cannot be measured by entry into the labour market alone; it must also be measured by the conditions under which women are allowed to participate.

What makes this especially unsettling is the contrast between the public language of respect for women and their everyday treatment. Celebrating women as mothers, goddesses or symbols of honour is not the same as respecting women as individuals. Reverence can coexist with restrictions. The idea of family honour is sometimes placed on women’s shoulders, as though their choices carry the reputation of everyone around them. A woman should not have to be considered sacred before her rights are taken seriously.

The experience is not identical for every woman. Caste, class, disability, religion and location affect exposure to discrimination and the resources available to resist it. A woman with private transport and a secure job may navigate public life differently from a domestic worker returning late on an unreliable bus route. Recognising these differences does not weaken the case for equality. It makes that case more realistic.

Meaningful change requires more than advice to be careful. Well-lit streets, dependable public transport, responsive complaint systems and accountable institutions can make everyday spaces safer. So can schools and families that teach boys and men about consent, respect and responsibility. Workplaces need fair hiring, credible grievance procedures, parental support and a culture in which caregiving is not treated as a woman’s task alone.

Women should not be asked to choose between dignity and independence, or safety and visibility. The measure of a fair society is not how strongly it claims to protect women, but how confidently women can inhabit its classrooms, offices, streets and parks on their own terms. The right to public life belongs to them, after sunset as much as before it.
